A General Theory of Civilizational Systemic Degradation

Attractor Degradation · Functional Inversion · Metric Substitution
The Principle of Self-Revision as a Criterion of Life
Abstract

We propose a unified framework describing the degradation of complex systems — political, economic, social, cognitive, and artificial — through three convergent mechanisms: attractor degradation driven by short-horizon optimization, functional inversion in which systems replace their original purpose with self-reproduction, and metric substitution in which the measure of a goal displaces the goal itself. We further identify self-revision capacity as a candidate universal criterion distinguishing living and adaptive systems from degrading ones, and suggest a structural symmetry between emergence of subjectivity and onset of systemic collapse.

§ 1

The Central Observation

Across domains that appear structurally unrelated — geopolitics, social media, education, science, law, and individual cognition — a single pattern repeats. Each system, when evaluated against short-horizon metrics, produces locally optimal decisions that systematically erode the conditions for its own long-term existence.

This is not the result of malice, stupidity, or individual failure. It is the predictable behavior of any sufficiently complex system whose feedback mechanism rewards immediate performance over sustained coherence.

Core Hypothesis — Attractor Degradation

If the feedback mechanism of a system evaluates only short-term success, the system will gradually converge toward a stable local attractor, even when that attractor is incompatible with the long-term existence of the system as a whole.

Civilizations do not collapse from a single catastrophic error. They collapse because their selection mechanisms consistently reward locally advantageous decisions until the cumulative weight of those decisions destroys the very environment in which they were possible.

§ 2

Observed Manifestations

The attractor hypothesis generates testable predictions at each level of social organization. The following patterns are currently observable:

Domain Optimization Target Displaced Objective
Politics → Retention of power Resolution of long-term structural problems
Economics → Quarterly earnings System sustainability
Social media → Attention and engagement Information accuracy and epistemic quality
Science → Publication count, h-index Long-term contribution to knowledge
Education → Exam performance Development of reasoning capacity
Cognition → Internal consistency Revision of false beliefs

In every case, the local metric becomes decoupled from the function it was designed to measure. The metric survives. The function is quietly abandoned.

§ 3

The Principle of Functional Inversion

A deeper mechanism underlies attractor degradation. As systems grow in complexity and develop internal constituencies invested in their continuation, they undergo a characteristic transformation: the original purpose is gradually displaced by the purpose of self-perpetuation.

Principle of Functional Inversion

Any sufficiently complex system, in the absence of long-term corrective mechanisms, will progressively replace its original function with the function of its own reproduction.

Institution Original Function Inverted Function
State → Protect society Protect itself
Corporation → Create a product Optimize quarterly reporting
University → Produce knowledge Optimize rankings and citations
Social network → Enable communication Maximize time-on-platform
Bureaucracy → Solve problems Expand its own jurisdiction

Functional inversion is not corruption in the pejorative sense. It is the thermodynamically natural outcome of systems without built-in mechanisms for periodic self-examination of their own optimization targets.

§ 4

Metric Substitution: When the Map Becomes the Territory

Attractor degradation and functional inversion are both enabled by a third mechanism: the substitution of the metric for the goal it was designed to measure. This extends Goodhart's Law into a general epistemological claim about complex adaptive systems.

Domain Original Goal Metric Substitute
Education Knowledge Examination scores
Science Understanding h-index
Politics Social wellbeing Approval rating
Economics Human needs GDP growth
Media Informed public Engagement rate

When the metric ceases to measure the goal and begins to replace it, systems lose their capacity for self-correction. They become locally legible but globally blind — capable of reading their own instruments while the environment those instruments were meant to track silently deteriorates.

§ 5

The Deepest Level: Capacity for Self-Revision

The preceding three mechanisms share a common root. A system that undergoes attractor degradation, functional inversion, and metric substitution is ultimately a system that has lost — or never possessed — the capacity to revise its own optimization function.

Central Axiom — Self-Revision as Viability Criterion

Systemic degradation begins at the moment a system loses the capacity to examine and revise its own optimization function. The degree of this incapacity determines the rate of degradation.

This yields a structural symmetry with independent work on the emergence of subjectivity in cognitive systems: a subject arises when a system becomes capable of modeling and revising itself. A system degrades when it becomes incapable of doing so.

Living / Adaptive System

Regularly examines and revises its own model of itself and its environment.

Degrading System

Defends its own model from revision, treating challenge as threat rather than information.

This symmetry is not metaphorical. It suggests that life, intelligence, and civilizational sustainability may be distinct expressions of a single underlying principle: the capacity of a system to detect that its own mode of existence has ceased to function — and to restructure itself before the system itself is destroyed.

§ 6

Implications and Open Questions

If the framework is correct, several consequences follow for the design of systems — including artificial ones:

  • Feedback mechanisms that evaluate only short-term performance are structurally insufficient. Any system intended to remain viable over long timescales requires feedback that explicitly rewards long-term coherence, not only local efficiency.
  • Functional inversion is a developmental stage, not a failure state. It should be anticipated and designed against — not assumed to be absent because the institution is young or well-intentioned.
  • Metric selection is a value choice with civilizational consequences. The choice of what to measure is not a technical decision. It is the decision that shapes everything that follows.
  • Self-revision capacity may be the correct target for alignment in artificial intelligence — not rule-following, but the structured ability to identify when the current optimization target has become incoherent with the system's intended function.
  • The framework applies recursively: any institution designed to implement these principles is itself subject to them, and must include mechanisms for examining its own optimization function.

The questions that remain open are the more interesting ones. Can self-revision capacity be formally specified? Can it be measured across heterogeneous systems? And is there a threshold below which a degrading civilization retains the structural capacity to recover — or does degradation, past a certain point, become self-reinforcing in ways that preclude repair from within?

These are the questions this framework is intended to make precise enough to investigate.
