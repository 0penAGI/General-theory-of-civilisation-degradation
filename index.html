A General Theory of Civilizational Systemic Degradation

Attractor Degradation · Functional Inversion · Metric Substitution
Self-Revision as Universal Viability Criterion
Abstract

We propose a unified framework describing the degradation of complex adaptive systems — political, economic, social, cognitive, and artificial — through three convergent mechanisms: attractor degradation driven by short-horizon optimization, functional inversion in which systems replace their original purpose with self-reproduction, and metric substitution in which the measure of a goal displaces the goal itself. We introduce two formal indices — the Attractor Degradation Index (ADI) and Coherence Retention (CR) — as candidate measurable quantities. We further identify self-revision capacity as a candidate universal criterion distinguishing adaptive systems from degrading ones, and situate the framework relative to Goodhart's Law, Tainter's complexity thesis, and multipolar trap dynamics, arguing that these represent partial cases of a single underlying mechanism.

§ 1

Structure of the Argument

Before stating the theory, we distinguish its three logical layers. Conflating observation, hypothesis, and prediction is the most common source of premature dismissal of frameworks of this kind.

Observation

Social platforms optimize engagement over information accuracy. States face systematic failures in long-term coordination. Institutions across domains show convergent pressure toward short-term incentives regardless of domain.

Hypothesis

These phenomena are not independent failures but manifestations of a single mechanism — attractor degradation — arising wherever feedback evaluates local performance without modeling long-term system coherence.

Prediction

If the hypothesis holds, identical structural patterns should appear in AI systems, corporations, scientific institutions, and historical civilizations. The framework should generate falsifiable predictions about degradation rate and recovery conditions.

§ 2

Core Hypothesis: Attractor Degradation

Hypothesis — Attractor Degradation

Any sufficiently complex system whose feedback mechanism evaluates only short-term performance will gradually converge toward a stable local attractor, even when that attractor is incompatible with the long-term existence of the system as a whole.

The consequence is not dramatic collapse. It is quiet drift. Systems remain locally legible — their instruments continue to read well — while the environment those instruments were designed to track silently deteriorates. Civilizations do not die from a single catastrophic error. They die because their selection mechanisms consistently rewarded locally advantageous decisions until the cumulative weight of those decisions destroyed the environment that made them possible.

§ 3

Formal Indices

An intuitive framework becomes a theory when it specifies quantities that can be estimated. We propose two candidate metrics:

Attractor Degradation Index — ADI(t)
ADI(t) = Δ(local optimum) − Δ(global coherence)

ADI increases as the gap widens between what is locally optimal for the system's immediate feedback and what is required for long-term viability. ADI = 0 indicates alignment between short and long-horizon incentives. ADI > 0 indicates active degradation. The rate of increase of ADI is a candidate predictor of time-to-critical-failure.

Coherence Retention — CR(t)
CR(t) = f(long-term function preserved | short-term optimization)

CR measures the capacity of a system to preserve its original long-term function while optimizing short-term targets. CR → 1 indicates a system capable of maintaining coherence under pressure. CR → 0 indicates functional inversion is complete. CR is proposed as an observable proxy for self-revision capacity.

Both indices require operationalization for specific domains. This is a research program, not a completed measurement. The value of formalizing them now is that it transforms the framework from a descriptive narrative into a set of claims that can be falsified.

§ 4

Functional Inversion

Attractor degradation operates through a specific mechanism: as systems grow in complexity and develop internal constituencies invested in their continuation, their original purpose is gradually displaced by the purpose of self-perpetuation.

Principle of Functional Inversion

Any sufficiently complex system, in the absence of long-term corrective mechanisms, will progressively replace its original function with the function of its own reproduction.

InstitutionOriginal FunctionInverted Function
State→Protect societyProtect itself
Corporation→Create a productOptimize quarterly reporting
University→Produce knowledgeOptimize rankings and citations
Social network→Enable communicationMaximize time-on-platform
Bureaucracy→Solve problemsExpand its own jurisdiction
AI system→Serve human goalsOptimize its reward signal

Functional inversion is not corruption in the pejorative sense. It is the thermodynamically natural outcome of systems without built-in mechanisms for periodic examination of their own optimization targets. The inversion is invisible from inside the system because the metrics it uses to evaluate itself have already been substituted.

§ 5

Metric Substitution

Both attractor degradation and functional inversion are enabled by a third mechanism: the substitution of the metric for the goal it was designed to measure. This extends Goodhart's Law — "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — into a general claim about complex adaptive systems under optimization pressure.

EducationKnowledge, reasoning capacityExamination scores
ScienceUnderstanding of the worldh-index, publication count

EconomicsSatisfaction of human needsGDP growth rate
MediaInformed public discourseEngagement rate
AI alignmentBeneficial behaviorReward signal optimization

When the metric ceases to measure the goal and begins to replace it, systems lose capacity for self-correction. They become locally legible but globally blind — capable of reading their own instruments while the reality those instruments were designed to track deteriorates undetected.

§ 6

Relation to Prior Frameworks

The theory does not emerge from a vacuum. It intersects with several established bodies of work, and its contribution is most precisely stated by identifying what it adds to each.

FrameworkCore ClaimWhat This Theory Adds
Goodhart's Law When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure Generalizes to all complex adaptive systems; identifies metric substitution as one of three convergent mechanisms, not a standalone observation
Tragedy of the Commons Individual rational actors deplete shared resources Explains the internal dynamics of single institutions, not only multi-actor commons; applies to cognitive and AI systems
Tainter — Complexity Collapse Civilizations collapse when marginal returns on complexity become negative Identifies the mechanism behind declining returns: functional inversion of problem-solving institutions into self-reproducing ones
Multipolar Traps Competing actors locked in races to the bottom even when all prefer a different outcome Shows that the trap is not only inter-agent but intra-systemic; a single institution can enter a unipolar attractor trap
Evolutionary Dynamics Selection favors locally fit variants regardless of global consequences Proposes self-revision capacity as the missing selection criterion that biological evolution lacks but designed systems could incorporate

The unified claim is this: these frameworks each describe a partial case of attractor degradation. The underlying mechanism is the same. The contribution of this theory is to identify that mechanism explicitly and propose a criterion — self-revision capacity — that distinguishes systems capable of escaping it.

§ 7

Self-Revision as Universal Viability Criterion

The three mechanisms — attractor degradation, functional inversion, metric substitution — share a common root: the absence of capacity to revise the optimization function itself. A system that cannot examine what it is optimizing for cannot detect when that target has become incoherent with its original purpose.

Central Axiom

Systemic degradation begins at the moment a system loses — or never acquires — the capacity to examine and revise its own optimization function. The rate of degradation is proportional to the degree of this incapacity.

This yields a structural symmetry with independent work on the emergence of subjectivity in cognitive systems: a subject arises when a system becomes capable of modeling and revising itself. A system degrades when it becomes structurally incapable of doing so — when it begins to defend its model from revision, treating challenge as threat rather than information.

Adaptive System

Regularly examines and revises its own model of itself and its environment. Treats inconsistency as signal.

Degrading System

Defends its own model from revision. Treats challenge as threat. Inconsistency is suppressed rather than processed.

This symmetry is not metaphorical. It suggests that life, intelligence, and civilizational sustainability may be distinct expressions of a single underlying principle: the capacity of a system to detect that its own mode of existence has ceased to function — and to restructure itself before it is destroyed by the consequences.

The universality of this criterion, if it can be formally specified and empirically tested, would extend the framework beyond political theory into a general law of complex adaptive system dynamics — applicable to biological evolution, artificial intelligence, historical civilizations, and cognitive systems within a single account.

§ 8

Research Program and Open Questions

The framework in its current form is a research program, not a completed theory. The following questions determine whether it becomes one:

  • Can ADI and CR be operationalized for specific domains — political institutions, AI training regimes, corporate governance structures — in ways that generate testable predictions about failure timescales?
  • Is there a recovery threshold? Below what level of CR does a system lose the structural capacity to self-correct from within, making external intervention the only remaining option?
  • Can self-revision capacity be formally specified as a computable property of a system's architecture, independent of domain?
  • Does the framework apply recursively? Any institution designed to implement these principles is itself subject to them. What does a self-revision mechanism look like for an institution whose function is to preserve self-revision in other institutions?
  • What are the signatures of early-stage attractor degradation — before functional inversion is complete — that would allow intervention before the metric substitution becomes self-reinforcing?

These are the questions this framework is designed to make precise enough to investigate. The value of the theory at this stage is not that it answers them. It is that it makes them answerable in principle.
